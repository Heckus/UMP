\section{Motivation and Background}
\label{sec:motivation}
Coral reefs represent some of the most ecologically vital and biodiverse marine ecosystems on Earth, providing habitat for over a quarter of all marine species and offering critical coastal protection and economic value~\cite{souter2020status}. However, these delicate ecosystems are facing unprecedented threats from global climate change, marine heatwaves leading to mass bleaching, severe cyclonic storms, and anthropogenic runoff. Timely, high-precision monitoring of coral structures is paramount to evaluate reef recovery, assess the efficacy of ecological interventions, and detect physical habitat destruction~\cite{icri2021monitoring}.

Historically, benthic ecological monitoring has relied on manual diver-based surveys. Established methodologies—such as Point Intercept Transects (PIT), Manta Tows, and fixed-site photo-quadrats—rely predominantly on two-dimensional planar imagery~\cite{icri2021monitoring}. As highlighted in recent critical surveys of reef condition metrics~\cite{davey2025review}, 2D surface metrics (such as planar percentage coral cover) fail to capture three-dimensional geometric complexity, structural rugosity, colony volume, or structural breakage under overhanging reef canopies. While \gls{sfm} photogrammetry has made inroads into marine surveys, generating dense multi-temporal point clouds remains computationally expensive, slow to optimize, and prone to severe degradation from water-column turbidity.

The recent emergence of \gls{3dgs}~\cite{10897713} has transformed three-dimensional computer vision, offering real-time rendering speeds and explicit volumetric scene representations. Nonetheless, adapting \gls{3dgs} to marine environments poses acute scientific challenges:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Wavelength-Dependent Optical Degradation:} Water rapidly absorbs longer wavelengths of light (red spectrum), resulting in severe blue-green colour casts, while forward- and back-scattering off suspended organic particulate matter creates pervasive haze, contrast degradation, and caustic illumination patterns~\cite{10203316, 11128502}.
    \item \textbf{Floating Primitive Artifacts:} Standard \gls{3dgs} optimizers compensate for water haze and caustics by generating millions of spurious, low-opacity ``floating'' Gaussians within the empty water column, severely corrupting true scene geometry~\cite{guedon2024sugar}.
    \item \textbf{The Scene Change Detection Dilemma:} When multi-temporal surveys are compared across different tidal states, varying turbidity and seasonal ambient lighting cause naive change detection algorithms to falsely flag water-column variations as physical structural shifts~\cite{galappaththige2026pixelsprimitivesscenechange}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Project Research Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}
This research project, undertaken at the \gls{qcr}, has a clear primary goal: \textbf{identifying physical scene change directly using 3D Gaussian splats} via primitive-space \gls{scd} in underwater environments. Supporting this primary goal, the project also produces and reviews distinct 3D surface models (\texttt{.obj}) across candidate underwater \gls{3dgs} methods. The specific research objectives are structured as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Primary Goal --- Primitive-Space Scene Change Detection on 3D Gaussian Splats:} Evaluate and extend the efficacy of \gls{oscd}~\cite{galappaththige2026pixelsprimitivesscenechange} operating directly in 3D Gaussian primitive space (comparing spatial centroids, covariance matrices, and spherical harmonic colours) to identify scene changes without relying on viewpoint-dependent 2D image renders.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation of Underwater 3DGS Representations:} Benchmark candidate underwater \gls{3dgs} architectures across physical inverse-rendering models (e.g., \textit{SeaSplat}~\cite{11128502}, \textit{3D-UIR}~\cite{huang2025restorationreconstructionrethinking3d}), restoration-guided frameworks (\textit{Gaussian Splashing}~\cite{mualem2025gaussiansplashingdirectvolumetric}, \textit{WaterSplatting}~\cite{li2025watersplattingfastunderwater3d}), and supplementary methods (\textit{RUSplatting}~\cite{jiang2025rusplattingrobust3dgaussian}, \textit{UW-GS}~\cite{10943785}) to determine which method produces the cleanest, most geometrically accurate 3D foundation under marine optical degradation.
    \item \textbf{Per-Model 3D Surface Extraction and Review via SuGaR (Supporting Output \& Bonus Feature):} The foundational 3D change detection paper~\cite{galappaththige2026pixelsprimitivesscenechange} explicitly recommended integrating the \gls{sugar}~\cite{guedon2024sugar} surface regularization method at the end of their paper to bind 3D Gaussians to physical surfaces and mitigate Gaussian drift. Motivated by this recommendation, we integrate \gls{sugar} to generate and review \textbf{distinct 3D surface models (\texttt{.obj} with UV-baked \texttt{.mtl}/\texttt{.png} textures) for every evaluated underwater \gls{3dgs} method right now}. Because ecological reef monitoring often requires identifying fine-scale \textbf{millimetre-to-centimetre ($\text{mm} \to \text{cm}$) changes in coral}, reviewing the 3D models produced by each underwater method allows us to investigate whether different underwater \gls{3dgs} formulations yield cleaner, more accurate 3D reconstructions that can support $\text{mm} \to \text{cm}$ change detection now or as a future direction.
    \item \textbf{Development of an End-to-End HPC Automation Pipeline:} Design, provision, and validate an automated 6-stage batch execution suite on the QUT Aqua \gls{hpc} cluster to support large-scale marine photogrammetry, per-model 3D mesh extraction, and interactive local review.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Project Scope, Proof-of-Concept, and the EcoRRAP Horizon}
\label{sec:scope_context}
A vital contextual dimension of this research is the staged progression between the current benchmark phase and the ultimate project goal:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Current Proof-of-Concept Phase (3D Splats + Per-Model 3D Meshes):} To systematically establish baseline metrics, test algorithmic implementations, and solve cluster compatibility across 11 isolated environments, the current work utilises public underwater benchmark datasets (\textit{Submerged3D} Kwajalein and Tokai Maru shipwrecks~\cite{11128502}) alongside a custom-captured dual-state change detection dataset (\texttt{Custom\_OSCD\_Dataset}). All 15 planned benchmark targets have achieved 100\% full-lifecycle execution, producing both native 3D Gaussian splats (\texttt{.ply}) and model-specific 3D surface meshes (\texttt{.obj}) that are actively reviewed in Blender and MeshLab.
    \item \textbf{The Core Research Goal (AIMS EcoRRAP \& $\text{mm} \to \text{cm}$ Coral Change):} While the open-source datasets validate the computational pipeline, the primary ecological use case targets high-density temporal coral monitoring photogrammetry provided by the \gls{aims} \gls{ecorrap} project~\cite{gordon2023field}. The EcoRRAP dataset encompasses a massive 1.35~TB repository of multi-temporal coral reef surveys, where biological changes (such as branching tip growth, plating accretion, or skeletal erosion) frequently occur at \textbf{millimetre-to-centimetre ($\text{mm} \to \text{cm}$) scales}. Currently, data acquisition is undergoing formal inter-institutional data-sharing and legal compliance review.
    \item \textbf{The Architectural Bridge:} While identifying change directly on the 3D Gaussian splats remains the main objective, extracting and reviewing 3D \texttt{.obj} models for each underwater method right now serves as a critical supporting feature. By comparing which underwater 3DGS method produces cleaner, floater-free 3D surface reconstructions via \gls{sugar}, we establish whether pairing a higher-fidelity 3D reconstruction model with \gls{oscd} can better resolve $\text{mm} \to \text{cm}$ coral changes once the 1.35~TB EcoRRAP dataset is deployed on QUT Aqua.
\end{itemize}

\section{The 5-Phase Operational Lifecycle}
\label{sec:lifecycle}
The research methodology is governed by a rigorous 5-phase operational lifecycle, depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:lifecycle_diagram}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Phase 1: Setup:} Provision 11 isolated Conda environments, compile custom CUDA rasterization kernels, and perform pre-flight hardware verification.
    \item \textbf{Phase 2: Run:} Orchestrate the 6-stage pipeline (Data Ingestion, COLMAP SfM, Monocular Depth Estimation, Model Optimization, Per-Model SuGaR 3D Mesh Extraction, and Quantitative Benchmarking).
    \item \textbf{Phase 3: Extract:} Bundle verified outputs (including per-model \texttt{.ply} splats and \texttt{.obj}/\texttt{.mtl}/\texttt{.png} 3D meshes) using dynamic shell manifests and transfer archives via the QUT Data Transfer Node (\texttt{eresdtn01.qut.edu.au}).
    \item \textbf{Phase 4: Organise:} Standardize raw artifacts into a presentation-ready academic hierarchy with automatic per-model mesh matching and \texttt{.mtl} texture binding.
    \item \textbf{Phase 5: Review \& Evaluate:} Synthesize cross-model benchmark tables, inspect 3D volumetric splats and thermal change heatmaps in WebGL/CloudCompare, and review per-model 3D \texttt{.obj} meshes in Blender and MeshLab.
\end{enumerate}
